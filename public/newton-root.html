Finding Square Roots without a Calculator

Imagine you are back in the 17th century. You need to calculate \(\sqrt{S}\). Perfect squares like 16 or 25 are easy enough to guess, but what about something like \(\sqrt{12}\)? You only have pen and paper.

Isaac Newton (and Joseph Raphson) popularized a brilliant method for approximating roots of functions. We can adapt this to find square roots incredibly quickly.

Reframing the Problem

We want to find \(x = \sqrt{S}\).

We can rewrite this equation: square both sides to get \(x^2 = S\), and then move everything to one side:

\(f(x) = x^2 - S = 0\)

Finding the square root of \(S\) is exactly the same as finding where the parabola \(f(x) = x^2 - S\) crosses the x-axis (its root).

Look at the graph on the right. The white curve is our function. The point where it crosses the horizontal line is our answer.

Taking a Guess

Newton’s method requires us to start with an initial guess, let’s call it \(x_0\).

Let’s pick an arbitrary starting point on the x-axis. It doesn’t have to be perfect, just somewhere in the ballpark.

We go straight up from our guess \(x_0\) to see where it lands on the curve. This point is \((x_0, f(x_0))\).

The Magic Tangent

Here is the core idea of Newton’s method: locally, a curve looks like a straight line.

If we draw the tangent line to the curve exactly at our point \((x_0, f(x_0))\), it approximates the curve near that point.

Calculus tells us the slope of this tangent line is the derivative of the function, \(f'(x) = 2x\).

Sliding Down to the Next Guess

Instead of trying to find where the curvy parabola hits zero (which is hard), we find where the straight tangent line hits zero (which is easy!).

We follow the tangent line down to the x-axis. The point where it crosses becomes our new, much better guess: \(x_1\).

The formula for this jump is:
\(x_{new} = x_{old} - \frac{f(x_{old})}{f'(x_{old})}\)
For square roots, this simplifies beautifully to:
\(x_1 = \frac{1}{2}(x_0 + \frac{S}{x_0})\)

Notice how much closer \(x_1\) is to the actual root than \(x_0\) was!

Rinse and Repeat

We now repeat the process. We start from \(x_1\), go up to the curve, draw a new tangent line, and follow it down to find \(x_2\).

Quadratic Convergence

Newton’s method exhibits quadratic convergence. This means roughly the number of correct digits doubles with every step!

What started as a geometric intuition—riding tangent lines down to zero—turns out to be one of the most powerful and ubiquitous numerical algorithms in all of mathematics and computer science.
