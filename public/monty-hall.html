The Monty Hall Problem

A classic problem in probability that continues to confuse intuition. Scroll to unpack the math.

The Setup

Imagine you're on a game show. Before you are three identical doors.

Behind one door is a brand new car 🚗. Behind the other two doors are goats 🐐.

You want the car. The host asks you to pick a door.

Your Choice

Let's say you pick Door No. 1.

At this point, you have a 1/3 chance of having picked the car, and a 2/3 chance of having picked a goat.

Simple enough.

The Twist

The host, who knows what is behind every door, doesn't open your door right away.

Instead, he walks over to the other two unchosen doors and opens one of them to reveal a goat.

(He can always do this, because there are two goats. Even if you picked a goat, there is still one goat left among the other doors).

The Question

The host turns to you and asks: "Do you want to stick with your original choice, or do you want to switch to the other closed door?"

What should you do? Does it matter?

The Intuitive Trap

Most people's intuition screams: "It doesn't matter! There are two doors left, one has a car, one has a goat. It's a 50/50 chance."

This feels right because we usually assume unknown options are equally likely.

But the host's action was not random. Let's look at the probabilities.

The Original State

Let's group the doors. Originally, your chosen Door 1 had a 1/3 chance of containing the car.

The other group (Doors 2 and 3 combined) had a 2/3 chance.

The Host's Constraint

When the host opens a door with a goat, he is giving you information about that 2/3 group.

He is essentially saying: "If the car is in this group of two doors (which happens 66% of the time), it's specifically behind this closed one."

The 2/3 probability doesn't disappear; it collapses onto the remaining closed door.

Listing the Outcomes

Let's look at all possible scenarios if you choose Door 1 and decide to switch.

  • Scenario A: Car is behind Door 1. Host opens 2 or 3. You switch. You Lose. (Happens 1/3 of the time)
  • Scenario B: Car is behind Door 2. Host must open Door 3. You switch to 2. You Win! (Happens 1/3 of the time)
  • Scenario C: Car is behind Door 3. Host must open Door 2. You switch to 3. You Win! (Happens 1/3 of the time)

If you switch, you win in 2 out of 3 scenarios.

The Conclusion

By switching, you are essentially betting that your original choice was wrong.

Since your original choice was a goat 2/3 of the time, betting against it wins 2/3 of the time.

You should always switch. It doubles your chances of winning from 33.3% to 66.7%.

Exaggerating the Problem

If 3 doors still feel like a 50/50 trick, let's exaggerate the scenario to build better intuition.

Imagine there are 9 doors instead of 3. Behind one is a car; behind 8 are goats.

The Long Shot

You randomly pick Door No. 1.

Your chance of guessing right on the first try is exactly 1/9.

The chance that the car is hidden somewhere among the other 8 doors is a massive 8/9.

The Host's Purge

The host, who knows exactly where the car is, now opens 7 of those other doors to reveal 7 goats.

He leaves only your door and one other door (say, Door No. 7) closed.

The Obvious Switch

He asks: "Do you want to switch?" Now the illusion breaks.

It's highly improbable that you guessed right on your ~11% first try. The host just did the hard work of filtering out the 7 goats from the 8/9 group.

That remaining door inherited the entire probability. It holds an 8/9 (about 89%) chance. You definitely switch.
