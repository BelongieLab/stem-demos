Towers of Hanoi

Welcome to an exploration of one of computer science's most famous puzzles. The Towers of Hanoi isn't just a game; it's a profound window into recursion, exponential growth, and surprisingly, binary counting.

The Rules

You have three pegs and N disks of different sizes. They start neatly stacked on the left peg, from largest at the bottom to smallest at the top.

The goal is to move the entire stack to the right peg, obeying two simple rules:

  • Move only one disk at a time.
  • Never place a larger disk onto a smaller disk.

The Recursive Insight

How do we move a stack of 4 disks? It seems daunting. But what if we knew how to move 3 disks?

To move a stack of N disks from Source to Target:

  1. Move the top N-1 disks from Source to the Auxiliary peg. (We trust our recursive function to handle this smaller problem).
  2. Move the massive Nth disk (the bottom one) directly from Source to Target.
  3. Move those N-1 disks from Auxiliary onto the Target, resting them on the big disk.

This "leap of faith" is the essence of recursion. We break a large problem into smaller versions of itself until we reach a base case (moving 1 disk is trivial).

function hanoi(n, source, target, aux) {
    if (n === 0) return;
    
    // 1. Move n-1 disks out of the way
    hanoi(n - 1, source, aux, target);
    
    // 2. Move the largest disk
    moveDisk(source, target);
    
    // 3. Move n-1 disks onto the target
    hanoi(n - 1, aux, target, source);
}

The Binary Connection

There is a beautiful, hidden structure here. Let's look at the disks. Label them from smallest (disk 0) to largest (disk N-1).

Watch the disks as they move. The smallest disk moves every alternate step (steps 1, 3, 5...). The next disk moves every 4 steps (steps 2, 6, 10...). Disk k moves at step 2k and then every 2k+1 steps.

If we write the step number in binary, the position of the least significant 1-bit tells us exactly which disk to move!

  • Step 1 (001): Move Disk 0
  • Step 2 (010): Move Disk 1
  • Step 3 (011): Move Disk 0
  • Step 4 (100): Move Disk 2

Furthermore, the total number of minimum moves required for N disks is exactly 2N - 1. A tower of 64 disks, as in the ancient legend, would take 264 - 1 moves—billions of years even at one move per second.
