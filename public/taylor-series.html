The Power of Polynomials

Functions like \(\sin(x)\), \(\cos(x)\), or \(e^x\) are essential in mathematics, but how do computers actually calculate them?

Computers only really know how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide. Enter the Taylor Series: a brilliant mathematical tool that lets us approximate complicated functions using only polynomials.

Let our target function be \(f(x)\). Our goal is to build a polynomial approximation, step-by-step, which we will call \(P(x)\).

The Target: Sine Wave

Let's start with a target function: \(f(x) = \sin(x)\). This is the blue dashed line on the right.

Our goal is to build a polynomial that mimics this wave around a specific point, often called the center. We'll use \(x = 0\) (Maclaurin series) as our center, marked by the yellow dot.

Degree 0: Just a point

The simplest approximation is a flat horizontal line (a degree 0 polynomial).

We want our approximation, let's call it \(P(x)\), to exactly match the value of \(f(x)\) at our center point \(x = 0\).

$$P_0(x) = f(0) = \sin(0) = 0$$

It's a terrible approximation everywhere except exactly at \(x = 0\).

Degree 1: Matching the Slope

To make it better, let's match the slope (the first derivative) at \(x=0\).

The derivative of \(\sin(x)\) is \(\cos(x)\), and \(\cos(0) = 1\). So we add a term with a slope of 1.

$$P_1(x) = 0 + \frac{f'(0)}{1!}x = x$$

This is the tangent line. It's a good approximation near the center, but it diverges quickly.

Degree 3: Adding Curvature

Let's match the second derivative. For sine, \(f''(0) = -\sin(0) = 0\). So the \(x^2\) term is zero!

Moving to the third derivative: \(f'''(0) = -\cos(0) = -1\). We add an \(x^3\) term, divided by 3! (3 factorial).

$$P_3(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{3!}$$

Notice how the pink line now bends to "hug" the sine wave much further out!

Degree 5: Reversing Direction

Because all even derivatives of sine at 0 are zero, our series only has odd powers.

The signs alternate. The next term pulls the curve back up.

$$P_5(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!}$$

Degree 13: A Remarkable Fit

As we keep adding terms, dividing by increasingly huge factorials, the polynomial begins to perfectly map over the sine wave.

$$P_{13}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{6} \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)!} x^{2n+1}$$

This is how a calculator computes \(\sin(x)\). It doesn't draw triangles; it computes a carefully chosen polynomial.

The Radius of Convergence

For \(f(x) = \sin(x)\), if we added an infinite number of terms, it would match the function perfectly for every real number. We say its radius of convergence is infinite.

Not all functions are so cooperative. Some series only converge near the center point. But for Sine, Cosine, and \(e^x\), this beautiful convergence holds true everywhere.

The Perils of High Degrees

While Taylor series are elegant, forcing a single high-degree polynomial to fit a function over a wide range can sometimes lead to wild oscillations at the edges—a trap known as Runge's phenomenon.

These issues won't be covered here, but because of them, applied mathematics and scientific computing often rely on other fascinating kinds of approximations. Techniques using Chebyshev polynomials, Padé approximants (rational functions), or splines (piecewise polynomials) are frequently preferred for robust, real-world computational stability.
