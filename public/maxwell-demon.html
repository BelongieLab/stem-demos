Maxwell's Demon

A thought experiment that challenged the undisputed law of the universe.

The Second Law

The Second Law of Thermodynamics is one of the most fundamental rules of physics. It states that the total entropy (a measure of disorder or randomness) of an isolated system can never decrease over time.

If you drop an egg, it breaks. It never spontaneously reassembles. Heat flows from hot to cold, never the reverse. Things naturally tend towards chaos.

A Box of Gas

Imagine a sealed box divided into two halves by a wall. The box is filled with a gas.

At the microscopic level, this gas consists of countless particles bouncing around randomly. In a gas at equilibrium, particles are moving at various speeds—some fast (hot) and some slow (cold)—evenly distributed throughout both halves.

The Challenger Appears

In 1867, physicist James Clerk Maxwell proposed a thought experiment to 'pick a hole' in the Second Law.

He imagined a tiny, intelligent being—later dubbed "Maxwell's Demon"—guarding a microscopic, frictionless trapdoor in the wall between the two halves of the box.

Sorting Chaos

The Demon observes the speed of every particle approaching the door.

When a fast-moving (hot) particle approaches from the left, the Demon opens the door to let it into the right chamber. When a slow-moving (cold) particle approaches from the right, it lets it into the left chamber.

Breaking the Law

Over time, the right side accumulates fast particles (getting hotter), and the left side accumulates slow particles (getting colder).

The Demon has created a temperature difference out of randomness without doing any physical work (the door is frictionless). This decreases total entropy, seemingly violating the Second Law!

The Resolution

For decades, this paradox troubled physicists. The resolution came through information theory.

To sort particles, the Demon must measure their speed and store that information. Eventually, its memory gets full. To continue, it must erase memory. Physicist Rolf Landauer proved that erasing information inherently produces heat and increases entropy, saving the Second Law.
